Unpack the Question

Case study: the Great Fire of Rome, AD 64

Hubert Robert, The Fire of Rome, 1785 (detail). Public domain.  ·  Teacher: press N for notes, → to advance.

Before the lesson: one Case Study Pack and one Lesson 1 worksheet per student; sticky notes; Blu Tack.

This four-lesson sequence needs no prior knowledge — every student, from any class, starts equal. The case study is only the vehicle; the real content is extended-response writing.

Keys: → / space next step · ← back · K timer · O all slides · L colour legend · T light/dark · 1–7 toggle a colour.

Do Now · gesture vote · thumbs up = true, thumbs down = false

True or false?

  • 1“Nero fiddled while Rome burned.” FALSE — the fiddle (violin) wasn’t invented until the 1500s. Ancient writers say he sang … and even that is disputed.
  • 2The fire destroyed or badly damaged most of Rome. TRUE — of 14 districts, 3 were levelled and 7 left as half-burnt ruins (Tacitus).
  • 3Nero was in Rome when the fire started. FALSE — he was at Antium, about 50 km away (Tacitus).
  • 4Historians agree that Nero started the fire. FALSE — it is still debated. That debate is our lesson.

Read each statement; all students vote at once (thumbs up / down) before you reveal. Scan the room — this tells you what myths they bring.

Don’t teach yet; just reveal. Keep to 4 minutes.

The big idea

If the evidence is weak, why does everyone “know” Nero did it?

Answering that takes more than facts. It takes an extended response: weighing evidence and making a judgement.

Pose the question; take two or three quick answers (think–pair–share, 60 seconds). Accept anything plausible: rumour, hostile writers, his reputation for cruelty.

Then reveal: this is exactly what an extended response does — it weighs evidence and lands on a judgement.

The question we will answer

To what extent was Nero responsible for the Great Fire of Rome in AD 64?

Each lesson builds one part of the response. By Lesson 4 every student writes a full extended response under time — and checks it against success criteria.

I can

Build the field · worksheet Part A — fill the gaps as we go

The story in six moments

19 July AD 64
Fire breaks out in shops full of flammable goods at the Circus Maximus.
Days 1–5
Wind drives it through narrow, winding streets. Nero is at Antium.
Nero returns
Only when it reaches his own house — which burns. He opens shelters, brings food, cuts grain prices.
Day 6
Stopped by a firebreak — then restarts on land owned by Tigellinus, Nero’s guard commander.
AD 64–68
Nero builds the Golden House on burnt land; blames the Christians.
AD 65 · AD 68
An officer calls him an “incendiary”. Three years later Nero is dead.

Narrate each card (about 1 minute each) as students complete the cloze in Part A. The bold words are the cloze answers: Antium · firebreak · Tigellinus · Golden House · Christians · incendiary.

EAL/D: point to each card as you say the bold word; have students repeat it.

Look closely

Painted in 1785 — 1,700 years after the fire. Every source we use was made by someone, at a time, for a reason.

Ask: “Is this evidence of what the fire looked like?” (No — it is an 18th-century imagining.) Use it to plant the habit for Lesson 2: always ask who made a source, when, and why.

I do · watch, then copy into Part B

To what extent was Nero responsible for the Great Fire of Rome in AD 64?

Directive — the thinking demanded

“To what extent” = how far? A judgement of degree, weighed against the counter-evidence.

Key term — must be defined or tested

“Responsible” can mean more than one thing. Defining it opens up your argument.

Scope — who, what, when

Nero · the Great Fire · AD 64 — and its aftermath, because that shaped who was blamed.

Think aloud as each colour appears. Script: “First I find the directive — the words that tell me what thinking to do. ‘To what extent’ … so I can’t just say yes or no; I must say how far.”

“Next, the key term. ‘Responsible’ — that word is doing a lot of work. I need to pin it down.”

“Scope: Nero, this fire, AD 64. Is the Golden House in scope? He built it after AD 64 — but it’s part of why people blamed him, so I’ll include it.”

I do · the directive

“To what extent” means: land on the line — and prove it

Entirely
Largely
Partly
Minimally
Not at all
◄ Nero more responsibleNero less responsible ►
NESA: assess

“Make a judgement of value, quality, outcomes, results or size.”

NESA: evaluate

“Make a judgement based on criteria; determine the value of.”

“To what extent” behaves like assess/evaluate: a judgement of degree. A response that only lists evidence for and against — without landing anywhere — cannot reach the top band.

I do · the key term

Nero could be “responsible” in four ways

Started it

Did he cause it — directly or by giving orders?

Made it worse

Did his actions or absence let it spread?

Exploited it

Did he use the disaster for his own benefit?

Was blamed for it

Is he responsible for the belief in his guilt?

A sophisticated answer can say: not responsible in one sense, but responsible in another.

Think aloud: “If I only use meaning 1, my answer is a simple yes/no. If I separate the meanings, I can argue something more interesting — maybe he didn’t start it, but he exploited it.” Students record these in the Part B table.

We do · worksheet Part C · cold call

Assess the reliability of the ancient sources for Nero’s role in the Great Fire of Rome.

Find the directive, key terms and scope with your partner — 2 minutes

What it demands

A judgement of how reliable the sources are — not a summary of what they say.

Pairs annotate Part C (2 min). Then cold call (wait 3–5 seconds before naming a student): “What is the directive? … What must we define? … Which sources are in scope?” Reveal each colour after an answer.

Answers — Directive: Assess. Key terms: reliability; Nero’s role. Scope: the ancient sources (Tacitus, Suetonius, Dio, Subrius Flavus); the Great Fire.

Hinge question · hold up 1, 2, 3 or 4 fingers

Which opening sentence actually answers “to what extent”?

  • 1In AD 64 a terrible fire broke out in Rome and burned for many days.
  • 2Nero was a cruel emperor who had his own mother killed.
  • 3Nero was minimally responsible for starting the fire, but largely responsible for exploiting it.
  • 4There are many ancient sources about the Great Fire of Rome.

Only 3 makes a judgement of degree. 1 narrates · 2 is off-question · 4 describes the sources.

Everyone votes at once. If fewer than about 80% choose 3, re-teach with the extent scale slide before moving on. Common error: choosing 1 — narrating instead of judging.

You do · worksheet Part D · 12 minutes

Sort the evidence: TO Nero, or AWAY from him?

Foundation

Tick TO or AWAY for all 12. Circle the strongest card on each side and finish the frames.

Standard

Sort all 12. Star the two strongest each side. Explain why one starred card beats an unstarred one.

HPGE

Also classify by type of responsibility and rate reliability. Which type has the strongest evidence? Which cards are ambiguous?

Use your Case Study Pack. Press K for the timer.

Circulate. Look for students who tick card 7 (torches) as definite proof — prompt: “What else does Tacitus say the men might have been doing?” (looting).

There is no single correct sort — reward reasoning. Cards 7 and 12 are ambiguous; 5 can be argued as ‘exploited’ (winning popularity). Full key in the Teacher Guide.

Your working position

entirelylargelysubstantiallypartlyto a limited extentminimallynot at all
Example

Nero was minimally responsible for starting the fire because he was at Antium and his own house burned.

It’s a working position — students can change it after Lesson 2’s source work. Push Standard/HPGE students to name which kind of responsibility they mean.
