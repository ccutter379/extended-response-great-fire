Supported reading · Source A · follow in your Case Study Pack

A disaster followed, whether accidental or treacherously contrived by the emperor, is uncertain … others openly hurled brands, and kept shouting that there was one who gave them authority, either seeking to plunder more freely, or obeying orders.

Tacitus, Annals 15.38 · written c. AD 115–120

Think aloud

Tacitus — no friend of Nero — says it is uncertain. That’s a careful historian hedging.

Think aloud

He offers two explanations for the torches: looting or orders. So the torches don’t prove Nero did it.

Modelled read-aloud: read the extract aloud slowly while students follow. Stop at each highlight and think aloud (script on the slide). Then ask: “If Tacitus isn’t sure, what should we make of writers who are very sure?”

I do · from position to thesis

A thesis is your answer — with its reasoning showing

Not a fact

The Great Fire began on 19 July AD 64.

Not an announcement

This essay will talk about Nero and the fire.

Not a bare opinion

Nero was a terrible emperor.

Thesis = topic + controlling idea

A judgement of degree on the question, with the main reason — and, for a strong thesis, a concession to the other side.

Writing in Secondary calls this “topic + controlling idea”: the topic is the question’s subject; the controlling idea is the judgement that steers the whole essay.

I do · the thesis ladder · think aloud

Weak

Nero was responsible for the Great Fire.

No degree, no reason, no counter-view.

Better

Nero was largely not responsible for the Great Fire because he was at Antium when it started.

Degree + one reason. Still no counter-view.

Best

Although Suetonius and Cassius Dio accuse Nero of arson, the weight of evidence suggests that he did not start the Great Fire; his responsibility lies instead in exploiting its aftermath, which made the accusation believable.

Think aloud up the ladder: “Weak — I can’t tell how far or why. Better — now I have ‘largely not’ and a reason. Best — I admit the other side first (‘Although…’), I use cautious modality (‘the weight of evidence suggests’), and I redefine responsibility, which gives me a more interesting argument.”

I do · the formula (on your worksheet)

Although , the weight of evidence suggests that Nero was responsible for because .

Extent words
entirelylargelypartlyminimallynot at all
Modality
the weight of evidence suggestsit is likely thatarguablyclearlyit is improbable that

Modality = how certain you sound. On a contested question, controlled modality (‘the weight of evidence suggests’) is stronger than false certainty (‘Nero definitely…’).

Hinge question · 1, 2, 3 or 4 fingers

Which of these is a thesis?

  • 1The Great Fire began on 19 July AD 64.
  • 2This essay will talk about Nero and the fire.
  • 3Nero was a terrible emperor.
  • 4While Nero exploited the fire’s aftermath, the evidence that he started it is late and hostile.

4 judges, concedes and gives a reason. 1 = fact · 2 = announcement · 3 = opinion with no degree or reason.

If fewer than about 80% choose 4, return to the “What is a thesis?” slide before the introduction model.

I do · the introduction

Four moves

Move 1
Context

Set the scene in one sentence.

Move 2
Key term

Define or open up “responsible”.

Move 3
Thesis

Degree + reason + concession.

Move 4
Roadmap

Preview the line of argument — never “This essay will…”.

The thesis is highlighted because it’s the heart of the introduction — everything else frames it.

I do · What A Good One Looks Like · worksheet Part C

1The conflagration that swept through Rome in July AD 64, levelling three of the city’s fourteen districts and gutting seven more, has long been laid at the feet of the emperor Nero. 2Yet ‘responsibility’ for a disaster can take more than one form: a ruler may be culpable for igniting it, for failing to contain it, or for exploiting its ruins. 3Although Suetonius and Cassius Dio present Nero as a deliberate arsonist, the weight of the evidence suggests that he did not start the fire; his responsibility lies instead in his opportunistic exploitation of its aftermath, which rendered the accusation of arson credible to contemporaries and to the hostile historical tradition that followed. 4Weighing the fire’s probable accidental origins against the late and partisan testimony of the ancient writers, and against Nero’s conduct once the flames had died, reveals an emperor less guilty of the crime than of its political consequences.

Reveal one move at a time and think aloud:

  • 1 Context — note the nominalisation “conflagration” and the precise detail (3 of 14 districts).
  • 2 Key term — three meanings of responsibility set up the argument.
  • 3 Thesis — concession (purple), then judgement (red) with modality: “the weight of the evidence suggests”.
  • 4 Roadmap — previews the three body paragraphs (origins · hostile sources · aftermath) without “This essay will”. Ends decisively.

Students complete the “Which move?” table on the worksheet.

Language spotlight · nominalisation · cold call

Turn the action into a “thing”

EverydayAcademic
The fire spread rapidly.The rapid spread of the fire…
Nero responded generously.Nero’s generous response…
Romans suspected Nero.Roman suspicion of Nero…

Why bother? A noun can become the subject of a new sentence — so you can argue about it: “Nero’s generous response, however, failed to silence the rumour.”
